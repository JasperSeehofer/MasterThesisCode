Chapter 4 — The Universe Only Shows You Its Loud Half

If I don’t divide by what I could have seen, my posterior doesn’t get noisy — it runs to the edge of the prior and stops caring what the truth is.

The failure this chapter answers Chapter 3 built a likelihood: sum over every catalogue galaxy in the localization ball, weight each by how likely it is to host an EMRI, take the ratio of sums. It is a defensible object. Run it on 1588 real detections — and it peaks at h = 0.600, the bottom of the prior grid. Not near the truth. Not inside the plausible range. Below it, hard against the wall.

A wrong answer that is noisy is a statistics problem. A wrong answer that is railed — pinned to the boundary of the prior, with the posterior mean equal to the lower edge to six significant figures — is a physics problem. The likelihood is not merely uncertain about $h$; it is monotonically confident in a direction, and it would happily go further if the grid let it.

Something in that likelihood is not a likelihood. This chapter finds it, names it, and hands you the switch.

1. What did we condition on?

Write down, in words, what Chapter 3’s likelihood actually says. For event $i$ it says: given a Hubble parameter $h$, how probable is this measured distance and sky position, if the source sat in one of these candidate galaxies? It is the probability of the data given that an EMRI happened.

But that is not the experiment. You did not observe “an EMRI happened”. You observed “an EMRI happened and LISA’s pipeline recovered it above threshold”. Those are different events, and the second one is not a neutral filter on the first: detection prefers loud sources, loud means nearby, and “nearby” is exactly the thing $h$ is supposed to be inferred from. The selection is correlated with the parameter. That is the whole problem.

The conditioning rule RATIFIED G2c D3 Mandel, Farr & Gair 2019, arXiv:1809.02063 When events enter the analysis only if they pass a detection criterion, the correct per-event likelihood is the detected-data likelihood: the unconditioned likelihood divided by the probability that a source drawn from the population would have been detected at all, $$ p(x_i \mid h,\ \text{detected}) \;=\; \frac{p(x_i \mid h)}{p(\mathcal{D} \mid h)} , \qquad p(\mathcal{D}\mid h) \;=\; \int p_{\rm det}\,(\text{population}) . $$ The denominator is called the selection integral. It depends on $h$, it is the same for every event, and it does not cancel.

Two features of that denominator decide everything that follows. First, it is an integral over the whole population, not over this event’s neighbourhood: the question it answers is “of all the sources this universe could have produced, what fraction would I have seen?” Second, it is raised to the power of the number of events — each event divides by it once, so $N$ events divide by it $N$ times. With $N$ in the thousands, a denominator that is even mildly sloped in $h$ becomes an enormous monotone pull. Delete it, and you have not removed a small correction; you have removed the only thing standing between your posterior and the wall.

2. $p_{\rm det}$: a horizon you can measure

To divide by the probability of detection you must first have a probability of detection. Call it $p_{\rm det}$: given a source at luminosity distance $d_L$, what fraction of the sources the population model produces at that distance would clear the detection threshold? In this pipeline the threshold is a matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio of $\rho_{\rm thr} = 20$, applied to a deterministic optimal SNR (constants.py SNR_THRESHOLD).

You could try to model that. The project measures it instead, from an injection campaign: hundreds of thousands of simulated EMRIs, drawn from the population model, each pushed through the same waveform and response code the real analysis uses, each with its own SNR. And then one observation makes the whole thing cheap.

The detection-horizon survival estimator RATIFIED simulation_detection_probability.py Finn & Chernoff 1993, arXiv:gr-qc/9301003 Finn 1996, arXiv:gr-qc/9601048 Gravitational-wave strain amplitude falls as $1/d_L$, so injection $k$ — recorded with SNR $\rho_k$ at distance $d_{L,k}$ — would sit exactly at threshold if it were moved to its detection horizon $$ d_{{\rm hor},k} \;=\; \frac{\rho_k\, d_{L,k}}{\rho_{\rm thr}} . $$ Detection is then simply “is the source closer than its own horizon?”, and the detection probability is exactly the survival function of the horizon distribution: $$ p_{\rm det}(d_L) \;=\; \Pr\bigl(d_{\rm hor} \ge d_L\bigr) \;=\; \text{fraction of injections still detectable at } d_L . $$ No kernel, no fit, no smoothing parameter — a sorted array and a suffix count.

The elegance is in what is missing from that formula: $h$. The horizon set $\{d_{{\rm hor},k}\}$ is a property of the injections’ amplitudes, and when you re-map redshifts to distances with a different Hubble parameter, the $1/d_L$ amplitude scaling and the $d_L$ rescaling cancel. The survival grid is built once and reused for every trial $h$. This is not an argument in a docstring; it is checkable, and the chapter’s generator checks it: the estimator’s distance ceiling reads 9.164987 Gpc at $h = 0.60$, at $h = 0.73$ and at $h = 0.86$, and the survival curves at those $h$ differ by a maximum of 0.0 — bitwise identical arrays. The production run’s own log prints the same dl_max = 9.1650 Gpc on all 41 grid points.

Note also what this $p_{\rm det}$ is a function of: distance, and nothing else. If you come from LVK analyses you are used to a selection function $p_{\rm det}(\theta)$ — masses, spins, inclination, sky position — evaluated per event. The object above is a marginal: the intrinsic-parameter dependence has been integrated out over the population the injections were drawn from. That is what makes it cheap, and it is exact only if the events you apply it to are drawn from that same population. It is a modelling assumption, not a definition — and Chapter 9 measures a case where it does not hold, because the galaxies a catalogue contains are not a fair draw from the population the injections sampled ⏭ Ch 9.

Trap — “$p_{\rm det}$ is a detail of the simulation, not of the physics.” $p_{\rm det}$ appears in the denominator of every event’s likelihood and its $h$-dependence is a direct multiplicative tilt on the posterior. 3. $D(h)$, and why the whole volume

Now assemble the denominator — call it $D(h)$. Take the population’s expected distribution of sources in redshift $z$ — the comoving volume element $dV_c/dz\,d\Omega$, divided by $(1+z)$ for the source-to-detector time dilation — weight each shell by the fraction of sources there that would be detected, and integrate over everything:

The full-volume selection normalization RATIFIED G2c §2 row D(h) bayesian_statistics.py:1013 $$ \underbrace{D(h)}_{\text{Mpc}^3\,\text{sr}^{-1}} \;=\; \int_{z_{\min}}^{z_{\max}(h)} \bigl\langle p_{\rm det}\bigl(d_L(z,h),\Omega\bigr)\bigr\rangle_{\Omega}\; \frac{dV_c}{dz\,d\Omega}\;\frac{dz}{1+z} $$ with the sky average taken over equal-area HEALPix pixels. The population’s overall rate normalization and its mass integral are $z$-independent under this campaign’s surrogate and cancel between numerator and denominator, so only the volume and the time dilation survive here (Option A; G2c §3). Babak et al. (2017), arXiv:1703.09722 for the rate; Mandel, Farr & Gair (2019), arXiv:1809.02063 for the detector-frame rate density.
Citation correction — carried, not propagated FINDING G2c §6 C2 The precompute_completion_denominator docstring cites “Gray Eqs. 33 / A.19” for $D(h)$. That attribution is wrong: $D(h)$ carries no incompleteness weight, so it is the denominator of (A14) — the full-volume GW selection normalization $p(D_{\rm GW}\mid s, H_0)$ expanded — not the $\bar G$-restricted (A18)/(A19) object. The mathematics implemented is as documented; only the citation is stale, from an era when $D(h)$ normalized a different term. This book cites (A14) and says why.

Why the whole volume, though? The local version looks more consistent: the numerator sums over candidate galaxies inside one localization ball, so surely the denominator should sum over the same ball. It is a genuinely tempting mistake, and the project shipped it first.

The answer is that numerator and denominator are answering different questions. The numerator asks “given $h$, how well does this data match a source at these candidate hosts?” The denominator asks “given $h$, what fraction of all sources this universe could have produced would have been detected at all?” — a statement about the population, not about this event’s neighbourhood. Restrict it to the window and the normalization becomes event-dependent in a way that does not cancel across the stack, and every event contributes its own residual tilt.

Here is what $D(h)$ actually does across the grid, from the production run’s own log: it falls from $1.881\times10^{9}$ Mpc³ sr⁻¹ at $h = 0.60$ to $1.521\times10^{9}$ at $h = 0.73$ to $1.258\times10^{9}$ at $h = 0.86$ — a factor 1.4955 over the grid. Two effects fight, and the loser is worth knowing. Raising $h$ maps a given redshift to a smaller distance, so more of the population clears threshold there (at $z = 0.5$, $p_{\rm det}$ runs 0.285 at $h = 0.60$ to 0.553 at $h = 0.86$), and the fixed distance ceiling reaches to higher redshift ($z_{\max}$: 1.1334 at $h = 0.60$, and at the top grid point it would run past the analysis’s own redshift cap, so it is held at 1.5 — the same domain the candidate window uses, so that a truncation moves numerator and denominator together). Both push $D$ up. They lose anyway, because the comoving volume element itself carries $(c/H_0)^3 \propto h^{-3}$ — a factor 2.95 across this grid. Net: the visible universe shrinks by a third as you turn $h$ from 0.60 to 0.86, monotonically. Per event that is $\ln D(0.60) - \ln D(0.86) = 0.4025$. Multiply by 1588 events and the denominator is worth 639 nats of monotone pull across the grid.

For comparison: with $D(h)$ in place, the entire data-driven preference — the depth of $\sum_i \ln p_i(h)$ at its peak relative to the grid edges — is 18.8 nats (low edge) and 20.8 nats (high edge). The denominator you are about to delete is worth roughly thirty times the whole signal. That is why the failure mode is a rail and not a nudge: you are not perturbing the evidence, you are burying it.

4. Counted exactly once

There is a principle hiding in what you just did, and it governs the rest of this book. Every physical weight in the estimator — the volume prior, the rate weight, the detection probability, and later the redshift and mass kernels — must appear in the numerator and in the denominator exactly once. Not zero times, which is what deleting $D(h)$ does. And not twice.

The counted-once rule RATIFIED G2b §3.2 G6_starvation_postmortem.md:24-33 An estimator is prior-consistent when the same population prior multiplies the same measurement likelihood on both sides of the ratio. Every configuration in this project’s history that railed or “starved” violated that rule in one direction or the other: bare numerator with a bare global denominator, a $4\pi$-fixed numerator with a mismatched denominator, a numerator-only volume tilt, a smeared denominator with an unsmeared numerator. Consistency — not any single factor — is the cure.

Zero times and once you have now seen. Before twice, one loose end from Chapter 2 comes due. That chapter combined the per-event likelihoods into a joint posterior as a plain product — no population-level factor multiplied on top — and promised the justification here, because the justification is about $D(h)$ and Chapter 2 did not have $D(h)$ yet.

Why the product is plain — no extra $\beta(h)^N$ posterior_combination.py:284-330 Loredo 2004 Mandel, Farr & Gair 2019, arXiv:1809.02063 §3 Each $\mathcal{L}_i$ is already conditional on detection — that is what §1 installed — so it already carries one factor of $1/D(h)$. Conditioning on the observed number of detections makes the $\beta(h)^N$ factor of the unconditional (Poisson) likelihood cancel against the rate prior, so the joint posterior is the plain product $\prod_i \mathcal{L}_i$ and nothing further is owed. Applying $-N\log D(h)$ again on top is not conservatism; it is the same weight counted twice.

Twice is a real failure mode, and it has a receipt. In May the selection denominator was being applied both inside the per-event likelihood and again as an outer $-N\log D$ term. Removing the duplicate (6754ddb) eliminated a +0.020…+0.025 shift in $h$ and moved a closure test at a deliberately wrong truth from 0.6708 to 0.6555 — a pull of $+5.62\sigma$ down to $+1.67\sigma$ ledger #20. What makes this exhibit worth keeping is that the duplicate had been added on purpose, in an earlier phase, as a fix for a monotone $h$-pull (Phase 43-H1, ledger #15) — and was later found to be actively harmful. A correction that points the right way can still be a second copy of something you already had.

The same rule retired the most seductive negative result in the project’s history. In June the conclusion was formally recorded that in-catalogue photometric dark sirens are information-starved — that no normalization could recover a peaked $H_0$ from this data, because the information was not there. It was overturned two days later ledger #41 → #52: starvation was “a property of prior-inconsistent estimators, not of the data”. A negative result about your estimator is not a negative result about nature. The full exhibit is in the Defect Museum.

5. What the fixed estimator still cannot answer

So: conditioning repaired, denominator installed, posterior interior. In a mock universe where every host galaxy is in the catalogue, this estimator recovers the truth. Now look at what it is being asked to do here.

Of the 1588 detected events in this run, 76 have a host galaxy in the catalogue. The other 1512 do not — the source sits somewhere the catalogue has no entry at all. (The run’s parameter table lists 1590 rows, 76 of them in-catalogue; two rows carry no delivered posterior.)

Chapter 3’s likelihood is a sum over candidate hosts. For 95% of these events that sum is over the wrong galaxies, or over nothing at all. “Nothing at all” is not a figure of speech: rebuilding the candidate search at the production radius, 607 of the 1590 rows have no catalogue galaxy in the ball to sum over ch03 census, production 1.5σ ball. Read that number the way Chapter 3 asks you to — it is a reconstruction count on the truth catalogue, never a drop count: this run reports no event with a zero likelihood, so whatever it is doing with an empty ball, it is not discarding it. What, then, does your repaired estimator say about them?

Self-check

Q4.1   You delete the selection denominator entirely. Predict the direction of the bias in $h$ before reasoning about the formula, then check yourself.

Show answer
The undenominated likelihood over-rewards configurations where detection is easy — nearby sources — so it prefers $h$ values that make the observed distances correspond to low redshift, i.e. it rails low. The project measured exactly this: before the full-volume denominator, the MAP sat at 0.60, the bottom of the prior, with a bias of −17.8%; installing the correct $D(h)$ moved it to 0.73 and the bias to 0.0% ledger #9.

Q4.2   Why must the denominator be integrated over the full volume rather than over the same local candidate window as the numerator? The local version looks more “consistent”.

Show answer
Because the denominator answers a different question. The numerator asks “given this $h$, how well does this data match a source at these candidate hosts?”; the denominator asks “given this $h$, what fraction of all sources that could have occurred would have been detected at all?” — a question about the whole universe the population model describes, not about this event’s neighbourhood. Restricting it to the window makes the normalization event-dependent in a way that does not cancel, and the project measured that this single mis-scoping was the dominant bias of its era ledger #9.

Q4.3   $p_{\rm det}$ is estimated from a finite pool of injections. Name two distinct ways a finite pool can bias the inference rather than merely add noise.

Show answer
(i) Extrapolation policy. Filling $p_{\rm det}$ with 0 outside the grid silently deletes real support — the survival estimator then claims that sources in that band are undetectable rather than admitting it has no injections there. The project shipped exactly that and had to fix it explicitly (ledger #8, bias −9.2% → −6.9%). (ii) Support mismatch. If the pool is thin in the distance band where this run’s candidates actually live, $p_{\rm det}$ there is reconstructed from a handful of horizons, and a systematically low (or high) estimate in one band is not noise that averages away: it is a fixed tilt applied to every event that overlaps that band, and — because $D(h)$ is one number shared by all $N$ events — it enters the stack $N$ times, exactly like §3’s 639 nats. Ledger #8 is the extreme form of the same failure. Both mechanisms are systematic, not stochastic: more events do not cure either one.

Q4.4   The selection integral depends on $h$. Why does that dependence not simply cancel between numerator and denominator?

Show answer
Because they are integrals of different things over different supports: the numerator carries the GW measurement’s own likelihood evaluated at the candidate hosts, the denominator carries the population rate times detectability over the whole volume. Their $h$-dependences are genuinely different functions, and the ratio’s $h$-dependence is the selection correction. When a piece of it does cancel, that is a theorem worth proving, not an assumption — and the project has a gate that puts one such claimed cancellation to the test ⏭ Ch 9.

Q4.5 (transfer)   Your fixed estimator now recovers truth in a mock where every host is in the catalogue. In the real venue, only 76 of 1588 events have a catalogued host. What does your likelihood currently say about the other 1512?

Show answer
Nothing coherent — as written it either returns zero (if the candidate list is empty) or the likelihood of the wrong galaxies. The project actually shipped the “returns zero and the event is silently dropped” version, which discarded 58% of events on a deep venue before it was found and fixed ledger #54. The correct answer requires a second branch for uncatalogued hosts: Chapter 5.
Defect Museum · interlude

The $p_{\rm det}$ anchor REFUTED

The hypothesis
By May, with the denominator in place, a stubborn residual remained. $p_{\rm det}$ is built on a finite grid in $d_L$, and its very first bin — the $d_L \to 0$ asymptote, where detection should be certain — was measurably too low. That underestimate sits in the denominator of every event. It looked like an obvious suspect, and it was a real defect, not an imagined one.
The pre-registered prediction
Raise the anchor and the MAP moves toward truth. Plan 45-06 raised it from 0.7931 to 0.8873 — a +12% lift on the first bin of the selection function, applied to every event at once.
The measured outcome
The MAP did not move. Not “moved a little”: 1D 0.7550 and 2D 0.7450, unchanged, zero grid steps ledger #17 H0R:184-208, :1989-1990.
What it cost
Three plan cycles — 45-02/03, 45-04/05 and 45-06. The two shipped anchor fixes moved the production MAP by at most a single grid step; the decisive +12% escalation moved it by none.
The transferable lesson
The layer you can see is not the layer that matters. A quantity can be genuinely wrong, genuinely important-looking, and sit on top of a mechanism that integrates it away. Before you spend a cycle fixing something because it is visibly defective, ask what the answer is actually a function of — and prefer the test that can refute your suspect over the one that can only confirm it.
