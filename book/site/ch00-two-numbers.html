Chapter 0 — Two Numbers That Should Be One

Every rung of the local distance ladder is calibrated against another rung. There is no independent ruler.

Where this book starts Two groups measured the expansion rate of the same universe. Both worked for years, both quoted an uncertainty under 2%, both are careful. Their answers do not overlap — and no amount of staring at the two error bars decides which to believe.

That is the Hubble tension, and this book will not resolve it. It is about what resolving it would take — about the work hiding inside the phrase “a third, independent measurement”.

1. Two numbers, and what each of them actually is

They are not two attempts at the same measurement. They are two different measurements that ought to agree.

The lower one is not a distance measurement at all. It is an inference: the angular scale of the sound horizon frozen into the microwave background, propagated to today through a cosmological model. Change that model — extra relativistic species, early dark energy — and the number moves. It measures $H_0$, today’s expansion rate in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹, conditional on $\Lambda$CDM. arXiv:1807.06209

The upper one is a ladder. Parallaxes fix the distances to nearby Cepheids; Cepheids fix the luminosity of Type Ia supernovae; supernovae reach the smooth Hubble flow, where redshift means expansion, not local motion. Each rung is absolute only because the one below it was. arXiv:2112.04510

This is why “which one is wrong?” is the wrong question. If the ladder is wrong it is wrong in a rung — a metallicity dependence, a zero point — and a better ladder will not tell you. If it is right, the early-universe number is wrong because the model connecting then to now is incomplete: a discovery, not an error bar. Either way, what settles it is a measurement that fails differently.

The bottom row is this book’s own genre: gravitational-wave distances, no counterpart, redshift information from a galaxy catalogue. It stands at 10% of its own central value — 4.5× the precision ceiling §2 derives below arXiv:2111.03604. That is the state of the art; the rest of this book is what stands between it and a verdict.

Read the two time-delay rows together, because they are the same technique applied to the same class of systems. The published uncertainty went from about 2% to about 8% when one modelling assumption — the shape of the lens galaxy’s mass profile — was allowed to float. arXiv:1907.04869 arXiv:2007.02941 The extra 6% was there the whole time. It was not in the data; it was in the assumption, and it only became visible when somebody varied it.

That is the distinction this prologue exists to install. A number is a central value and an error bar. A measurement is a number plus a stated list of the ways it could be wrong, each one someone has tried to vary. The rest of this book is what the second kind costs.

2. What would actually arbitrate

Suppose you are designing that third method. The instinct is “better than the 8% gap”, and it is wrong twice over. A total 1σ has two parts: a statistical one, which shrinks as you collect events, and a systematic one, which does not. Park the method exactly on the early-universe anchor, demand a 3σ exclusion of the late-universe one, and the total is capped at 1.57 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹, 2.3% of $H_0$ — the more demanding placement; parked on the late-universe anchor the cap relaxes to 1.81. gen_ch00.py — closed form

Show me the algebra — the arbitration budget, written out
The book’s working definition of “arbitrate” toy: analytic

With $N$ events, each contributing an independent measurement of scale $\sigma_1$, $$\sigma_{\rm tot}^2 \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{N}}_{\text{statistical}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sigma_{\rm sys}^2}_{\text{does not shrink}},$$ and the significance with which the method separates itself from an anchor $A$ (published value $\mu_A$, uncertainty $\sigma_A$) is $$ T_A \;=\; \frac{|\mu-\mu_A|}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\rm tot}^2+\sigma_A^2}} . $$ The method arbitrates if it excludes one anchor at $T \ge 3$ while remaining consistent with the other at $T \le 2$. These are the book’s thresholds, chosen for this argument — not a project result, not a community standard.

The ceiling, in three steps. Parked exactly on one anchor, $|\mu-\mu_A|$ is the whole gap, so a 3σ exclusion of the far anchor demands $\sqrt{\sigma_{\rm tot}^2+\sigma_A^2} \le 5.64/3 = \mathord{}$1.88; subtract the far anchor’s own $\sigma_A$ in quadrature; what is left is the cap on $\sigma_{\rm tot}$. Which anchor you park on therefore matters, and the two branches are not equally kind — parked on the early-universe value the far $\sigma_A$ is 1.04 and the cap is 1.57; parked on the late-universe value it is 0.50 and the cap is 1.81. The page quotes the demanding branch throughout, and I0.1 below recomputes every one of these numbers in your browser and cross-checks itself against the generator. gen_ch00.py — closed form

So the honest answer to “how precise must it be” is 1–2%, not “better than 8%”: a method whose own 1σ is 8% holds both anchors inside its interval and arbitrates nothing. And notice where the ceiling sits — on the total. The statistical part you buy with events; the systematic part you cannot buy at any price, and if it alone exceeds the ceiling the method is dead before the first event.

Two hundred and forty times more events, and the second configuration still loses. That is not a quirk of these numbers; it is what the quadrature sum does. Once $\sigma_{\rm sys}$ dominates, $N$ has left the argument and every further event measures your systematic rather than $H_0$.

Why the systematics must be uncorrelated, not merely small

The second requirement is the one people skip: a discrepancy between two methods carries information only if their errors are independent. Build a third method on the ladder’s Cepheid zero point and agreement is circular — the same calibration measured twice — while disagreement is uninterpretable, the new physics being either in the universe or in the shared rung.

That is what makes gravitational-wave sirens structurally interesting, and the claim is worth stating exactly: the siren’s distance comes from the waveform amplitude and general relativity, sharing no rung with parallaxes, Cepheids or supernovae. That does not make it error-free. It makes its errors different errors — and there are a lot of them.

▸ For the GW reader

The toy above deliberately collapses the siren $H_0$ likelihood to a Gaussian of scale $\sigma_1/\sqrt N$, which is the standard forecast scaling for standard sirens (Schutz 1986, Nature 323, 310; and the $1/\sqrt{N}$ posture of every siren forecast since). The real object is not Gaussian and does not stack that way: in this pipeline’s own idealized baseline, 76 of 1588 detected events carry essentially all of the constraint and three of them carry 46% of it IDEALIZED_BASELINE_READOUT.md:42-47 ⏭ Ch 2. So “$N$ events” is already the wrong currency, and the widget’s $\sqrt N$ is the optimistic half of the story.

For a bright siren the redshift arrives with the counterpart. An EMRI has no counterpart, so the redshift has to be inferred from a galaxy catalogue — which is where this book’s entire systematic budget lives: catalogue completeness ⏭ Ch 5, photometric redshift widths ⏭ Ch 7, and the selection function of the detector itself ⏭ Ch 4. Those are the $\sigma_{\rm sys}$ of the method, and the honest position of this book is that they are not yet all measured.

3. The contract of this book

From here on the expansion rate is written in the dimensionless form the pipeline uses, $h = H_0 / (100\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}})$: the anchors sit at $h \approx 0.674$ and $h \approx 0.730$, and every posterior here is over that one scalar.

And every one of them is computed on a simulated universe whose answer is known in advance: h = 0.73, the value the mock population was generated with master_thesis_code/constants.py:25. That is the most important design decision here. On real data you can only compare your answer to somebody else’s; against a known truth bias is measurable and an estimator can be caught being wrong, not merely different.

The contract We are going to build a dark-siren estimator for $h$, one term per chapter, each term existing because the previous version visibly failed. Then we are going to spend most of the book attacking the finished object: deleting its terms, pushing its kernels past their validity, changing the units its masses are measured in, and running it over universes we built to be hostile. The chapters are named after the failures, not the formulas.

To make that felt rather than claimed, the book carries a permanent bias ledger rail — the strip beside this text (below it on a narrow screen) — showing the estimator’s measured bias in $h$, truth at zero. Right now it reads what it should: there is no estimator yet. That lasts until Chapter 3 builds one, whose first entry will be a failure.

One more promise. In the next chapter a single detected event opens a dossier that follows you to the last page, acquiring a host galaxy, a redshift, an error bar and, eventually, a warning label. By Chapter 11 it will be visibly capable of moving the headline number on its own: the whole book in one object, starting with an empty slot where its redshift should be.

Three conventions carry the book: every figure declares whether it is project data or teaching construction; the badges (RATIFIED through OPEN) are the project’s own, verbatim; ⏭ Ch 6 marks a tool not in use yet.

Self-check

Q0.1   The two $H_0$ values differ by about 8%. If one new method is to arbitrate, what fractional precision does it need — and why is “better than 8%” the wrong answer?

Show answer
It needs to be several times better than the gap — of order 1–2% — because arbitration requires distinguishing two hypotheses that are 8% apart with confidence, and a method whose own 1σ is 8% would place both values inside its own interval. The arithmetic in §2 puts the ceiling at 1.57 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ on the total uncertainty, 2.3% of $H_0$ gen_ch00.py — closed form. The deeper point is that precision is necessary but not sufficient: a 1% method with a 3% unknown systematic arbitrates nothing. This is why the rest of the book spends far more effort on bias than on σ.

Q0.2   Why does it matter that the new method’s systematics be uncorrelated with the ladder’s, rather than merely small?

Show answer
Because a discrepancy between two methods is only informative if their errors are independent. If a dark-siren $H_0$ inherited the same Cepheid or SN calibration as the ladder, agreement would be circular and disagreement uninterpretable. GW sirens are attractive precisely because the distance comes from the waveform amplitude and general relativity, sharing no calibration rung with the EM ladder — the systematics we spend this book on (catalogue completeness, photo-z, selection) are different systematics, which is the whole point.

Q0.3 (transfer)   $H_0 \approx cz/d_L$ needs a redshift, and the waveform gives only $d_L$; with no light it must be inferred. In §2’s budget, which side does that land on — and can more events buy you out?

Show answer
Both sides, but the dangerous half is $\sigma_{\rm sys}$. Each event’s own redshift guess is noisy, and that part does average down. What does not is the machinery the guess is made with — the catalogue, its coverage, the model of how it was assembled — because every event in the campaign is inferred through the same machinery, so its error is common to all of them and survives the average. By §2 a systematic above the ceiling is fatal at any $N$: collecting more events then measures the catalogue rather than $H_0$. That is why the chapters that follow are named after failure modes rather than after sample sizes, and it is the reason a method with no counterpart is a harder object than a method with one.
