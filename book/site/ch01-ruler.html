Chapter 1 — A Ruler That Needs No Ladder

General relativity gives $d_L$ absolutely — and says nothing at all about $z$.

The failure this chapter answers Every rung of the distance ladder is calibrated against the rung below it. Parallax calibrates Cepheids; Cepheids calibrate supernovae; supernovae reach into the Hubble flow. Nothing in that chain measures a distance from first principles — each step inherits every zero-point error beneath it, and the disagreement Chapter 0 opened with is, in the end, an argument about zero points.

A gravitational wave does not work that way. The amplitude of the strain that reaches the detector is fixed by general relativity, in terms of quantities the same waveform measures, with no free normalization. Point a detector at a binary and you get a distance in megaparsecs, calibrated by nothing.
The ladder
  1. parallax → nearby stars
  2. ↓ calibrates Cepheids
  3. ↓ calibrates SNe Ia
  4. ↓ reaches the Hubble flow

Four rungs, four zero points, one accumulated systematic.

The siren
  • strain amplitude $\propto 1/d_L$
  • constant of proportionality: GR

One step. No zero point to inherit.

That is the promise, and it is real. This chapter spends its first half making it precise, and its second half showing you the hole in it — a hole big enough that the remaining ten chapters are all about climbing out.

1. The waveform as a ruler

A compact binary radiates gravitational waves. Far from the source, the strain a detector sees is a product of three things: an amplitude set by the masses and the distance, a set of geometric factors from the orientation of the orbit and the position of the source on the sky, and a phase that winds forward as the orbit shrinks:

The standard-siren relation Schutz (1986), Nature 323, 310 derivations/dark_siren_likelihood.md §2.4 Gray et al. 2020, arXiv:1908.06050 $$ h_+(t) \;=\; \frac{\mathcal{A}(t)}{d_L}\,\frac{1+\cos^2\iota}{2}\,\cos\Phi(t), \qquad h_\times(t) \;=\; \frac{\mathcal{A}(t)}{d_L}\,\cos\iota\;\sin\Phi(t) , $$ with $\mathcal{A}$ fixed by the masses and the orbital frequency, $\iota$ the inclination of the orbital plane to the line of sight, and $\Phi$ the accumulated orbital phase. The luminosity distance $d_L$ enters as an overall $1/d_L$ — and there is no calibration constant anywhere in the expression. This is the entire content of the phrase “standard siren”.

Read that formula the way an experimentalist would and the trouble is immediate. You do not measure $h_+$ and $h_\times$ separately. You measure one number per detector channel — a projection $F_+h_+ + F_\times h_\times$ through the instrument’s antenna pattern — and its size depends on $d_L$, on $\cos\iota$, and on where the source sits on the sky, all multiplied together. A quiet source could be far away, or nearby and viewed edge-on. Amplitude alone cannot tell you which.

What breaks the tie is everything that is not amplitude. The phase $\Phi(t)$ is a function of the masses only; it does not care about distance or orientation at all, so it pins the masses independently. And because the detector moves — LISA cartwheels around the Sun once a year — the geometric factors $F_+$ and $F_\times$ are not constants but slow functions of time, and they mix the two polarizations in a ratio that changes as the mission proceeds. A pair $(d_L,\iota)$ that reproduces the observed amplitude today will generally not reproduce it six months from now.

So the distance is not read off; it is fitted, jointly with the masses and the geometry, out of a signal that is long enough for the geometry to rotate underneath it. Play with that below.

Trap — “An EMRI is just a long binary.” The length is the physics. About $10^5$ observable cycles deep in the strong field is why the redshifted mass $M_z$ is measured to $\sim10^{-4}$, and why the geometry has time to rotate through a full year of antenna pattern while the source is still inspiralling. A short binary — a few hundred cycles, a few seconds of visible geometry — has neither, and its distance is correspondingly poor. ▸ For the GW reader — where the amplitude actually gets weighed

The pipeline never manipulates $h(t)$ in the form printed above. It generates the response with few + fastlisaresponse and weighs everything in the noise-weighted inner product $$ \langle a \mid b\rangle \;=\; 4\,\mathrm{Re} \sum_{\alpha\in\{A,E\}} \int_{f_{\min}}^{f_{\max}} \frac{\tilde a_\alpha(f)\,\tilde b^{*}_\alpha(f)}{S_n(f)}\,df , \qquad \rho \;=\; \sqrt{\langle h\mid h\rangle} , $$ implemented in parameter_estimation.py:335 (scalar_product_of_functions) and :488 (compute_signal_to_noise_ratio), over the TDI A and E channels. The detection threshold is $\rho_{\rm thr} = 20$ (constants.py SNR_THRESHOLD).

Because $h \propto 1/d_L$ and the inner product is quadratic, $\rho \propto 1/d_L$ exactly — a fact Chapter 4 turns into a detection horizon and Chapter 6 turns into a covariance ⏭ Ch 4 ⏭ Ch 6. Two properties of $S_n(f)$ that this chapter does not need but that the reader should know exist: the galactic confusion foreground is in the PSD (Babak et al. 2023, arXiv:2303.15929 Eq. 17), and the discrete-to-continuous Fourier normalization of that integral once dropped a factor $dt^2$ — which multiplied every SNR by $1/10$ and shrank the detectable universe from $z \lesssim 1.5$ to $z \le 0.11$ (G8_dt2_inner_product_derivation.md, G7 row 1, ledger #51). Chapter 6 tells that story properly. ⏭ Ch 6

2. Meet the EMRI

The source this book is built on is an extreme mass-ratio inspiral: a stellar-mass compact object, $\mu \sim 10\,M_\odot$, spiralling into a massive black hole, $M \sim 10^5$–$10^7\,M_\odot$, at the centre of a galaxy. The mass ratio $\mu/M \sim 10^{-5}$ is what makes it extreme, and what makes it useful: the small body is a test particle, it radiates weakly, and it therefore takes years to fall in, executing of order $10^5$ orbits in LISA’s band before it plunges. Each of those orbits is a measurement.

An EMRI in this pipeline is described by fourteen numbers — two masses, the black hole’s spin, three numbers fixing the orbit at the start of observation, the luminosity distance, four angles fixing the sky position and the spin orientation, and three initial orbital phases. Twelve of them are nuisance; one of them is $d_L$; none of them is a redshift.

Sidebar — what counts as an “event” RATIFIED plunge_window_initial_conditions.md

A subtlety that will matter twice later. An event, in this pipeline, is not “an EMRI that is loud at some moment”. It is an EMRI that plunges during the observed mission span: the plunge time is drawn $t_{\rm plunge} \sim U[0, T]$ with $T = 4.5$ yr of science operations (Colpi et al. 2024, arXiv:2402.07571), and the orbit’s starting radius is then solved so that the inspiral takes exactly that long.

Two reasons, and they are the same reason twice. First, an EMRI’s signal-to-noise accumulates overwhelmingly in the last, strongest-field cycles, so the detectable population is essentially the plunging population. Second, the rate model these events are drawn from — Babak et al. (2017), arXiv:1703.09722, model M1 — is itself a plunge rate. Defining an event any other way makes the generator and the population model disagree about what they are counting, which is the first appearance in this book of a theme that eventually gets a chapter of its own. ⏭ Ch 9

The convention replaced an earlier draw, $p_0 \sim U[10,16]$, which was a waveform-code validity domain being used as an astrophysical prior — and which froze every high-mass event outside its detectable phase. Ratified 2026-07-28 under the project’s physics-change protocol.

3. The missing scale

You now have a distance in megaparsecs, obtained without a ladder. Turn it into a Hubble constant. At small redshift the recession velocity of a source is $cz$ and Hubble’s law says $v = H_0 d$, so

$$ H_0 \;\approx\; \frac{c\,z}{d_L} \qquad (z \ll 1). $$

One measured quantity on the right, one unmeasured one. The waveform delivered $d_L$ and, in delivering it, said nothing whatsoever about $z$ — a gravitational wave is not redshifted in a way you can detect from a single signal, because the redshift is perfectly degenerate with the masses: a heavier binary nearby and a lighter binary far away emit identical waveforms. What LISA measures is the redshifted mass $M_z = M(1+z)$, and without independent knowledge of $M$ you cannot separate the two. ⏭ Ch 8

Away from $z \ll 1$ the relation is not a division but an integral, and it acquires a second parameter:

The distance–redshift relation, flat ΛCDM derivations/dark_siren_likelihood.md §2.4 Eq. (14.7) physical_relations.py:132 dist Hogg (1999), arXiv:astro-ph/9905116 Eq. (16) $$ d_L(z;\,h,\Omega_m) \;=\; (1+z)\,\frac{c}{100\,h} \int_0^{z} \frac{dz'}{E(z')}, \qquad E(z) \;=\; \sqrt{\Omega_m (1+z)^3 + \Omega_\Lambda}, $$ with $\Omega_\Lambda = 1-\Omega_m$ (flat), $h = H_0/100\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$, and $c$ in km s$^{-1}$ so that $d_L$ comes out in Mpc. The pipeline evaluates the integral in closed form via a hypergeometric identity rather than by quadrature; the two agree to machine precision.

Look at where the two parameters sit. $h$ is a pure prefactor: it multiplies the whole curve by $1/h$ and changes no shape. $\Omega_m$ lives inside the integral: it bends the curve, and it can only bend it where there is enough path length to bend. That single structural difference is the reason the next widget looks the way it does — and the reason a nearby event like EMRI-889 is a clean probe of $h$ and a useless probe of $\Omega_m$.

Notice what just happened in that last readout, because it is the pivot of the whole book. To convert EMRI-889’s distance into a redshift you had to choose $h$ — the very number you were trying to measure. The ruler has no scale. Running it backwards is not a measurement; it is a restatement of your assumption.

Which means the redshift has to come from somewhere else entirely. And that is a different kind of problem, with a different kind of error bar.

4. Whose universe? The fiducial choice

There is a second thing you had to choose in that widget, and it is easier to miss. $\Omega_m$ was set to 0.2726. That is not Planck’s value (0.3153); it is not a typo; and it is not a leftover. It is a deliberate choice, and the reasoning is worth a paragraph because it is the first example in this book of the difference between a bug and a decision.

Why 0.2726 constants.py OMEGA_M GATE_SIGNOFF.md:26 (G11) ledger #53 Barausse et al. (2012), arXiv:1201.5888 The EMRI population these events are drawn from is the Barausse M1 model, whose black-hole mass function and event rates were computed in a specific cosmology. Adopting that cosmology as the mock universe’s truth makes the mock internally consistent: the same $\Omega_m$ generates the population, converts its redshifts to distances, and defines the volume the estimator normalizes over. Adopting Planck’s instead would put the population model and the distance model in disagreement — a real inconsistency, traded for a cosmetic one.

The honest cost of that choice is not zero, and the project quotes it rather than absorbing it. If the true universe were Planck’s and you analysed it assuming 0.2726, you would recover the wrong $H_0$ — by an amount that grows with redshift, exactly as §3 predicted, because $\Omega_m$ is a shape parameter with no leverage nearby:

The generator treats that as a hard gate and refuses to write a file if it ever drifts. Weighted over the detected population rather than read off at a single redshift, G7 row 6 quotes the same systematic as ≈+1.5–2.5%, with Ωm marginalization listed as future work.

Two things to take from the table. The first is scale: at the redshifts where this book’s information actually lives — EMRI-889 sits near $z \approx 0.02$ — the $\Omega_m$ systematic is well under a tenth of a percent, while the disagreement Chapter 0 opened with is about 8%. The second is the habit: the number is stated, in a budget, with the redshift dependence spelled out, rather than being described as “negligible” and forgotten.

▸ For the GW reader — the scope limit inside dist()

physical_relations.dist() accepts $w_0$ and $w_a$ in its signature but implements $\Lambda$CDM only (the comoving integral is evaluated with a hyp2f1 identity that assumes $w = -1$). Until the 2026-07-04 review it accepted non-default values and silently returned the $\Lambda$CDM answer; it now raises through _reject_unsupported_wcdm (physical_relations.py:36). Status: OPEN as a scope limit (GitHub #4), G7 row 14 — zero effect on this book, whose entire content is $w_0 = -1$, $w_a = 0$, and a loud failure rather than a quiet wrong answer for anyone who extends it.

Note also that dist() returns Gpc while the catalogue and most of the prose work in Mpc, and that the stored Cramér–Rao columns inherit the Gpc convention. That is the unit slip flagged in §2 above, and it is the reason the flag exists rather than a correction.

5. The gap, stated

Assemble what this chapter has and has not delivered. It has delivered a luminosity distance to a source 88.9 Mpc away, measured to a fraction of a percent, by an argument that begins with the Einstein field equations and ends at the detector with no calibration constant in between. That is a genuinely remarkable thing to be handed.

It has not delivered a redshift, and it cannot. A binary with an electromagnetic counterpart — a kilonova, an afterglow, anything that shines — hands you the host galaxy and its spectrum, and a spectrum is a redshift. That is a bright siren, and it is why GW170817 produced an $H_0$ measurement within a year of detection.

The gap An EMRI has no electromagnetic counterpart. The mass falling in is a single compact object; nothing disrupts, nothing radiates, and the event is invisible to every telescope ever built. A gravitational-wave source with a distance and no counterpart is a dark siren (Gray et al. 2020, arXiv:1908.06050). The redshift must be inferred, and this book is the cost of that inference.

There is one obvious way to proceed, and it is the right one. You know where the source is on the sky, to within some patch; you know how far away it is, to within some shell. Look up which catalogued galaxies lie inside that volume, and use their redshifts. It is cheap, the catalogues exist, and it is exactly what the rest of this book does.

It also smuggles in three assumptions, and the next several chapters are the successive discoveries that all three are false: that the host is in the catalogue ⏭ Ch 5; that it is identifiable among the candidates ⏭ Ch 3; and that it is at its catalogued redshift ⏭ Ch 7. Chapter 2 first has to say what “infer” even means when you have one distance, no redshift, and 1587 more events waiting.

Self-check

Q1.1   The waveform amplitude scales as $1/d_L$. Why does that not immediately mean the distance is measured as well as the amplitude?

Show answer
Because the observed amplitude is a product of distance, source masses, and the geometric factors of inclination and sky position folded through the detector’s antenna pattern. Amplitude alone is degenerate; the distance is recovered only because the phasing independently fixes the masses and the modulation of the signal across the mission fixes the geometry. That is why an EMRI’s $\sim10^5$ cycles are so valuable — they break the degeneracies that make a short binary’s distance poor. You built exactly this degeneracy in I1.1: the “degenerate twin” matches the recorded amplitude to machine precision at $t = 0$ and separates by tens of percent once the antenna pattern has rotated.

Q1.2   EMRI-889 has SNR 1425 and a fractional distance precision of $8.0\times10^{-5}$. Estimate what fractional $H_0$ precision that single event would give if its host redshift were known exactly, and say why the book will nevertheless not get anywhere near that.

Show answer
With $z$ exact, $\sigma_{H_0}/H_0 \approx \sigma_{d_L}/d_L \approx 0.008\%$ — an absurdly good single-event measurement, and indeed the idealized campaign’s total $\sigma_{H_0}/H_0 \approx 0.044\%$ is roughly $0.38\%/\sqrt{76}$, of the same character (IDEALIZED_BASELINE_READOUT.md:42-47). The book will not get near it because (i) the host redshift is not known exactly — it is a photometric redshift with median $\sigma_z/z \approx 49\%$, which dominates everything ⏭ Ch 7, and (ii) for 95% of events there is no identified host at all ⏭ Ch 5. The GW measurement is not the bottleneck; the galaxy catalogue is.

Editor’s note, and a live disagreement. The question’s $8.0\times10^{-5}$ is the number this book’s build spec carries; the stored row gives $\sigma_{d_L} = 7.984\times10^{-5}$ Gpc against $d_L = 0.0888792$ Gpc, i.e. a fraction of $8.983\times10^{-4}$ — see §2 and book/design/flags/ch01_FLAGS.md#F1. Redo the estimate with the recomputed fraction and you get $\sigma_{H_0}/H_0 \approx 0.09\%$ per golden event, which is the more interesting answer anyway: it is within a factor $\approx4$ of the $0.38\%$ per-event budget the idealized readout actually measured, whereas $0.008\%$ is a factor $\approx47$ away from it. The order-of-magnitude check you were asked to do is what catches the units slip — which is the real lesson, and a preview of Chapter 8, where an entire published MAP turns out to move when someone changes the units of a mass.

Q1.3   Why is “an event” defined in this pipeline as an EMRI that plunges inside the mission span, rather than one that is simply loud enough at some moment?

Show answer
Because the SNR of an EMRI accumulates overwhelmingly in the final, strongest-field cycles near plunge, so the detectable population is essentially the plunging population; and because the rate model the events are drawn from (Babak et al. 2017, arXiv:1703.09722, M1) is itself a plunge rate. Defining events any other way would put the generator and the population model in disagreement — the first instance of a theme that becomes Chapter 9. The concrete failure that forced the convention: the previous initial-condition draw ($p_0 \sim U[10,16]$, a waveform-code validity domain used as an astrophysical prior) froze every $M_z \gtrsim 10^{6.2}$ event outside its detectable plunge phase (plunge_window_initial_conditions.md, RATIFIED 2026-07-28).

Q1.4 (transfer)   You have $d_L$ to a fraction of a percent and no redshift. Sketch, in one sentence, the cheapest possible way to get a redshift, and name the assumption it smuggles in.

Show answer
Look up which galaxies lie inside the GW localization volume and use their redshifts — which smuggles in the assumptions that the host is (a) in the catalogue, (b) identifiable among the candidates, and (c) at its catalogued redshift. Chapters 3, 5 and 7 are the successive discoveries that all three are false. Note that even the word “volume” hides the circularity you met in I1.3: the shell you look inside is a shell in redshift, and mapping your measured distance into a redshift shell requires a value of $h$ — so the candidate set itself moves as $h$ moves. That is not a nuisance; in Chapter 3 it turns out to be the entire mechanism by which a galaxy catalogue constrains $H_0$ at all.
