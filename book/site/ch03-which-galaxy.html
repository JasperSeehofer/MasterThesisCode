Chapter 3 — Which Galaxy?

Not knowing the host is not an error bar — it is a sum, and the weights in that sum are astrophysics I have to commit to.

The failure this chapter answers Chapter 2 left you needing a per-event likelihood $\mathcal L_i(h)$. To build one you need a redshift, and the gravitational wave does not carry one — it carries a distance and a patch of sky. So you look the patch up in a galaxy catalogue. And then you have a new problem, which is not “my redshift is uncertain”. It is “I do not know which galaxy”, and that is a different kind of ignorance: discrete, not Gaussian.

The chapter’s running example, EMRI-889, has three galaxies in its ball and two after the window. Show me the numbers

So the honest statement of the problem is not “the host is uncertain”. It is: the host is one of $N$ specific objects, each of which has its own redshift, its own sky position, and its own astrophysical claim to being the kind of galaxy that hosts an EMRI — and $N$ ranges from 0 to 431,670. A parameter you do not know but which takes discrete values has exactly one correct treatment in Bayesian inference: you marginalize it.

1. The candidate ball

Marginalizing needs a list to sum over, and the list has to come from somewhere. The pipeline builds it geometrically: take the event’s Fisher sky uncertainty, turn it into a radius, and ask a spatial index for every catalogue galaxy inside.

The ball search RATIFIED galaxy_catalogue/handler.py:519 COORD-04 Catalogue galaxies are embedded as unit vectors on the sphere and indexed by a BallTree (handler.py:505), so the search metric is chord length. The 2×2 Fisher sky block $\Sigma$ is measured in $(\phi,\theta)$ coordinates, in which a step in $\phi$ is a great-circle step of $\sin\theta\,d\phi$ — so it is first rescaled, $$\Sigma' = J\,\Sigma\,J^{\mathsf T},\qquad J = \operatorname{diag}(|\sin\theta|,\,1),$$ and the search radius is $$ r \;=\; n_\sigma\,\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma')},\qquad n_\sigma = 2 , $$ the semi-major axis of the 2‑$\sigma$ error ellipse, as a chord on the unit sphere. Both the covariance and the catalogue are in the barycentric ecliptic frame (_coord_frame = ecliptic_BarycentricTrue_J2000); getting that wrong once cost this project a factor it could see — the equatorial/ecliptic migration moved the MAP 0.860 → 0.730 on its own ledger #12 ⏭ Ch 6.

That is the sky cut. Two more cuts follow, and the difference between them is one of the most load-bearing structural facts in this whole book.

The redshift window keeps a galaxy if its own $1\sigma$ redshift interval overlaps the redshift range the measured distance could correspond to (handler.py:584-592). Note which range: not the range for the current trial $h$, but the range spanned by the whole prior on $h$ — $z_{\min} = z(d_L - 3\sigma_{d_L};\,h = 0.60)$ to $z_{\max} = z(d_L + 3\sigma_{d_L};\,h = 0.86)$ (physical_relations.py:546, capped at the analysis’ redshift limit at bayesian_statistics.py:2826). The candidate list is therefore built once per event and is the same for every $h$. Remember that: when we sweep $h$ in a moment, the members of the sum will not change — only their weights.

The mass window keeps a galaxy if its black-hole mass interval is compatible with the event’s measured redshifted mass (handler.py:594-603). And here is the fact: the pipeline returns two candidate lists, one with the mass window applied and one without, and the channel this chapter is building — the distance-and-sky channel — uses the list without it (handler.py:592 vs :605). The mass window exists only in the second channel ⏭ Ch 8. When Chapter 8 asks “why was the 1D channel spared?”, this line is the answer, and the claim file records that forgetting it was an error of its own investigation.

Trap — “The right host is the closest galaxy to the best-fit sky position.”

Here the two leading candidates differ in separation by 1×10−5 arcmin: the nearest-neighbour rule is a coin flip on the book’s best-localized event of 1590. And the trap is subtler than “it is usually wrong”, because a measurement says something genuinely surprising. Evaluate the weighted numerator at the injected $h = 0.73$ on every event that has any candidate at all (1038 of 1590): 52.6% of them do have a single galaxy carrying more than half the sum, 35.3% have one carrying more than 90%, and the median effective number of contributing galaxies is 2.34. One galaxy often does dominate.

It is just not the nearest one. The galaxy that dominates is the one whose redshift puts it on the distance shell — sky proximity barely enters, because inside a ball defined by the sky uncertainty every member is, by construction, sky-compatible. And that dominance is an artefact of pretending each catalogue redshift is a number: the numbers above use the point-kernel limit, and EMRI-889’s real host is photometric ⏭ Ch 7. Structurally, too, the lookup is the wrong shape of answer: the likelihood is a weighted sum, and collapsing it discards the sky–distance correlation the Fisher matrix supplies ⏭ Ch 6.

2. Who hosts an EMRI?

Marginalizing a discrete latent means summing over its values weighted by their prior probability. So: what is the prior probability that galaxy $g$, rather than its neighbour, produced the extreme-mass-ratio inspiral you just detected?

The lazy answer is “all equal”. That answer is a physical claim, and it is false. An EMRI needs a massive black hole; the rate at which a given black hole captures a stellar-mass companion depends on its mass; and a fixed source-frame rate is diluted in the detector frame by time dilation. Both effects are standard and both are in the pipeline.

The rate weight RATIFIED G2c §2 row w_g bayesian_statistics.py:879 Babak et al. 2017, arXiv:1703.09722 $$ \underbrace{w_g}_{\text{relative, yr}^{-1}} \;=\; \frac{R_{\rm eff}(M_g)}{1 + z_g} $$ with $R_{\rm eff}(M)$ the effective per-MBH EMRI rate of the Barausse M1 population model (Babak et al. 2017, Eqs. 31 × 34, implemented in emri_rate.R_eff_per_mbh) evaluated at the catalogue’s source-frame black-hole mass $M_g$, and $1/(1+z_g)$ the source‑to‑detector rate dilation (Mandel, Farr & Gair 2019, arXiv:1809.02063). In Gray et al. (2020), arXiv:1908.06050 this slot holds a placeholder — luminosity weighting (A3) and a rate-evolution factor (A4); here it is filled with astrophysics. Any overall rescaling of $w$ cancels, because the weight enters numerator and denominator identically (bayesian_statistics.py:804).

The reason to care about this weight is not that it is large. Measured on EMRI-889’s two candidates, $R_{\rm eff}$ differs by 4% and the weights come out 88.50 and 92.14. That 4% does decide something — measured below, the host’s peak weighted numerator beats the impostor’s by 0.035 nats, and it does so entirely because of the rate weight: the host’s bare $N_g$ peak is actually 0.005 nats lower. It decides which of the two peaks is taller, and it comes nowhere near deciding which galaxy is the host, because the two peaks are at different values of $h$ in the first place.

The reason to care, then, is not the size of the weight. It is that this is the same weight the simulator used to draw the host in the first place (handler.py:765 draw_rate_weighted_hosts). Generator and estimator are holding the identical astrophysical opinion, by construction. That closes the loop — and it also means the loop can be opened, which is the subject of a later chapter and one of this project’s live inconsistencies ⏭ Ch 9.

A documented approximation, still open OPEN G2c §5 D1 sub-deviation In Gray’s (A10) the host-prior factors $p(s\mid z_i)$ sit inside the integral over the galaxy’s redshift. In the implementation $w_g$ is evaluated once, at the catalogue redshift $z_g$, and pulled outside that integral. The mapping document records this verbatim as “second-order for narrow $\sigma_z$ but not for GLADE+ photo‑$z$ widths — undocumented approximation”. It has not been measured. It is written down here because a book about a pipeline should say which of its steps are theorems and which are choices someone is still living with.

3. The ratio of sums

Now assemble the marginal. For each candidate galaxy $g$ you need two numbers, and it is worth being very clear about what each one answers.

Per-galaxy numerator and denominator RATIFIED G2c §2 rows N_g, D_g bayesian_statistics.py:3615 Gray et al. 2020, arXiv:1908.06050 (A.9)/(A.10)

“How well does the data fit a source sitting at $g$?” — $N_g$

$$ \underbrace{N_g}_{\text{sr}^{-1}} \;=\; \int \mathcal N_3\!\Bigl(\bigl(\phi_g,\ \theta_g,\ \tfrac{d_L(z,h)}{\hat d_L}\bigr);\, \bigl(\hat\phi,\hat\theta,1\bigr),\ \Sigma_3\Bigr)\; p_g(z)\, dz $$ — the event’s three-dimensional Fisher Gaussian in $(\phi,\theta)$ and the fractional distance $u = d_L/\hat d_L$, evaluated at the galaxy’s position, integrated against that galaxy’s redshift kernel $p_g(z)$. No $p_{\rm det}$ appears here (comment at bayesian_statistics.py:3722) — putting it in the numerator is a double-count, and this pipeline once did exactly that.

“How likely were you to have detected an EMRI at $g$ at all?” — $D_g$

$$ \underbrace{D_g}_{\text{dimensionless}} \;=\; \int p_{\rm det}\bigl(d_L(z,h),\,\Omega_g\bigr)\, p_g(z)\, dz $$ with $p_{\rm det}$ a measured detection probability. This chapter takes $p_{\rm det}$ as a given black box; building it, and discovering what happens when its population-level counterpart is missing, is the next chapter ⏭ Ch 4. The kernel $p_g(z)$ — the thing that turns “the catalogue says $z_g$” into a distribution — is Chapter 7 ⏭ Ch 7.

The in-catalogue likelihood $\mathcal L^{\rm cat}$ is then the ratio of the weighted sums:

$$ \boxed{\ \mathcal L^{\rm cat}(h) \;=\; \frac{\sum_g w_g\, N_g(h)}{\sum_g w_g\, D_g(h)}\ } $$ weighted_ratio_of_sums at bayesian_statistics.py:804; Gray et al. (2020) Eqs. (A.9)/(A.10) with the placeholder host weights replaced by $w_g$.

Everything about that expression is unremarkable except one thing, and the one thing is the parenthesis. It is a ratio of sums, not a sum of ratios: $$ \frac{\sum_g w_g N_g}{\sum_g w_g D_g} \qquad\text{versus}\qquad \frac{\sum_g w_g\,(N_g/D_g)}{\sum_g w_g} . $$ Both are averages of “fit divided by detectability” over the candidate set. Both reduce to the same thing when the $D_g$ are all equal. Neither is obviously wrong. They are not the same function, and the difference is not academic.

The mechanism is worth stating in words, because it is the reason the ratio-of-sums form is the right one and not merely the published one. The mean-of-ratios form divides each galaxy’s fit by its own detectability before averaging, so a candidate that is individually hard to detect has its contribution inflated — and as $D_g \to 0$ its term diverges. The ratio-of-sums form divides the total fit by the total detectability, so a galaxy you were unlikely to see contributes little to both sums and cannot dominate either. One of these is a well-posed average and the other needs an arbitrary floor to be finite at all. The full exhibit lives in the Defect Museum.

4. The mechanism: $h$ sweeps the shell

Here is the question this whole construction exists to answer, and if you take one thing from this book it should be this one. How does a galaxy catalogue know anything at all about $h$?

The gravitational wave never sees a redshift. It measures $\hat d_L$. A candidate galaxy never sees a distance; the catalogue gives $z_g$. The only thing that connects them is the cosmology, and in a flat ΛCDM with fixed $\Omega_m$ the connection is exactly inverse-linear in $h$: $$ d_L(z;h) \;=\; \frac{d_L(z;h=1)}{h} $$ because the whole $(1+z)\!\int\! dz'/E(z')$ integral is multiplied by $c/H_0 \propto 1/h$ (physical_relations.py:132; Hogg 1999, arXiv:astro-ph/9905116 Eq. 16). So each candidate galaxy makes exactly one statement about the Hubble parameter: $$ h^\star(g) \;=\; \frac{d_L(z_g;\,h=1)}{\hat d_L} $$ — “if I am the host, then $h$ is this.” Turning the $h$ dial does not change which galaxies are in the list. It changes which of them the measured distance shell is currently standing on. That sweep is the entire mechanism. If the catalogue were structureless — uniform galaxies at uniform redshifts — the sweep would find the same thing everywhere and carry no information at all.

5. Now run it

You have a complete per-event likelihood. It marginalizes the discrete host over a real candidate list, with an astrophysical prior on which galaxies host EMRIs, in the algebraic form the literature prescribes. Combine them in log space, exactly as Chapter 2 did — 1588 of the run’s 1590 Cramér–Rao rows carry a delivered posterior — and read off the maximum.

The failure the next chapter answers The posterior peaks at h = 0.60. That is the bottom edge of the prior grid: not near the truth, not inside the plausible range, hard against the wall — and it would go further if the grid let it. The measured bias of that estimator is −17.8% ledger #9 H0R:1980. Nothing in the derivation above is algebraically wrong. Something in it is conditioned wrong.

There is a hint already on this page, if you look at the single-event legs in I3.2 with the selection question in mind: event 676’s catalogue leg rails to the bottom of the grid and event 1121’s rails to the top. A quantity that is systematically largest wherever sources are easiest to detect is not a likelihood — it is a likelihood divided by the wrong thing. Chapter 4 names it.

Self-check

Q3.1   The likelihood sums over candidate hosts weighted by $w_g$. What happens to the inference if you set all $w_g$ equal?

Show answer
You assert that every galaxy in the ball is equally likely to host an EMRI, which is false: the EMRI rate per massive black hole depends on the black-hole mass, and the number of hosts per unit volume is not the number of events per unit volume. Equal weights therefore mis-state which redshifts the event could have come from — and, more subtly, they put the estimator into disagreement with the simulator, which drew the host using the rate weight (handler.py:765). That is the Chapter 9 failure mode in miniature. On EMRI-889 specifically the weights are 92.14 and 88.50, so the flat-prior error would be invisible; that is a property of this event, not of the choice.

Q3.2   As you increase $h$, the same measured $d_L$ maps to a larger $z$, so the candidate shell sweeps outward. Why is that sweep the entire mechanism by which a catalogue constrains H₀?

Show answer
Because the GW data never sees a redshift; it sees a distance. The only way $h$ can be preferred or disfavoured is if some values of $h$ place the shell on top of real galaxies and others place it in empty space. The likelihood is literally a measure of “how much galaxy is where this $h$ says the source must be”. If the catalogue were uniform and structureless — or if the shell were much wider than the structure — the sweep would carry no information, which is the quantitative content of Chapter 7’s photo-$z$ problem. Note also what the sweep does not do: it does not change the candidate list, which is built once over the whole $h$ prior (physical_relations.py:546). Only the weights move.

Q3.3   The in-catalogue likelihood is a ratio of sums, $\sum_g w_g N_g / \sum_g w_g D_g$, not a sum of ratios $\sum_g w_g (N_g/D_g)$. Why can that distinction not be waved away?

Show answer
Because the two differ whenever the per-galaxy denominators $D_g$ vary across the candidate set — which they do, since $D_g$ depends on each galaxy’s own redshift and detectability. The mean-of-ratios form over-weights galaxies that are individually hard to detect, and diverges as $D_g \to 0$, so it is not even finite without an arbitrary floor. The project shipped the wrong form; restoring Gray’s A.9/A.10 ratio of sums together with removing a spurious $p_{\rm det}$ from the numerator halved the 1D bias, 0.750 → 0.740 (ledger #26) — 0.010 in $h$ from two “obviously harmless” rearrangements, which no artifact in this project separates. Measured per event, the rearrangement alone moves the log-likelihood shape by 8×10−4 nats on EMRI-889 and 7.8×10−2 on event 676 (I3.2) — small per event, which is exactly why it survived review.

Q3.4   Order of magnitude: a well-localized EMRI at $z \approx 0.1$ with a ~10 deg² sky area and sub-percent distance precision. Roughly how many catalogued galaxies are in the ball, and what does that imply about the strength of a single event?

Show answer
Thousands to tens of thousands. Even with a razor-thin distance shell the angular uncertainty dominates the volume, and the catalogue’s density at low $z$ is high. Measured on this run: the median event holds 1616 galaxies in its sky ball and 12 after the redshift window; the 90th percentile holds 20,322 and 1708; event 1121 — 4.96° across — holds 57,535 and 26,652. A single event therefore rarely identifies a host; it supplies a weak preference over a large candidate set, and the constraint comes from stacking. Two caveats the measurement adds to that story. First, “large candidate set” does not imply “diffuse sum”: with point redshifts, one galaxy carries more than half the weighted numerator for 52.6% of the 1038 events that have any candidate, and the median effective number of contributors is 2.34 — concentration is the rule, and it is broken by the redshift kernel, not by the candidate count ⏭ Ch 7. Second, the exception at the other end: for EMRI-889 the ball holds three galaxies, which is exactly the regime in which 3 events carry 46% of the idealized constraint (IDEALIZED_BASELINE_READOUT.md:42-47).

Q3.5   Two galaxies sit in EMRI-889’s ball at the same sky separation to five significant figures, with rate weights within 4% of each other. One has a spectroscopic redshift with $\sigma_z = 2.6\times10^{-4}$; the other, the true host, has a photometric redshift with $\sigma_z = 4.4\times10^{-3}$. Which one contributes more to $\sum_g w_g N_g$ near the truth $h = 0.73$, and why is the answer uncomfortable?

Show answer
Near $h = 0.73$ the host dominates, because the shell is standing on it ($h^\star = 0.7294$) and nowhere near the impostor ($h^\star = 0.7675$). But that is a statement about a point redshift. The impostor’s spectroscopic $\sigma_z$ pins its vote to ±0.0089 in $h$, while the host’s photometric $\sigma_z$ smears its vote over ±0.155 — 236× the width the wave itself contributes. So the sharp, confident, wrong galaxy and the broad, vague, right galaxy are being added together, and which one wins depends entirely on how the pipeline turns “the catalogue says $z_g$” into a distribution. That choice has not been made yet in this chapter, and when it is made it will move the answer ⏭ Ch 7.

Q3.6 (transfer)   Run this estimator on the real event set and the posterior peaks at $h = 0.60$, the bottom of the prior. Before reading on: name one thing this likelihood conditions on incorrectly.

Show answer
It conditions on “an EMRI occurred at this galaxy”, when the data is “an EMRI occurred at this galaxy and we detected it”. Detection preferentially selects loud, nearby sources, so the likelihood as written rewards $h$ values that put sources where detection is easy. Correcting it requires dividing by the probability of detection integrated over everything the source could have been — Chapter 4. Note that $D_g$ is not that correction: it is a per-galaxy selection factor inside the ball, and the object that is missing is a population-level one.
